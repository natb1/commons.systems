## Answer

Against, and it is the strongest argument the tradition pass found against the disposition. The finding is that a judgment made by starting from a presented value and adjusting lands near that value, that the adjustment is insufficient, and that the effect survives knowing the anchor is arbitrary and survives being warned about it. Transposed to this page, every option row carrying the AI's recommendation is an anchor placed before the author's judgment, and the case against the recommended option, written by the party that recommends, is an adjustment offered by the anchor itself.

The disposition sharpens the problem rather than creating it. Under the fold rule the low-boldness decisions were not anchored, because they were not shown: they were taken by the AI and never put to the author at all. Listing every fact and every option converts those into decisions the author makes, each with a recommendation marked beside it, so the surface an anchor works on grows with the count of facts. On legitimacy that is a plain gain and the reading does not dispute it; on fidelity it trades silent AI decisions for anchored author decisions, and the trade is real.

The tradition therefore does not support the fold, and the reading is careful not to let it be read that way. An unshown decision is not an unanchored decision; it is a decision the author did not make. What the finding argues for is a guard on the anchoring, not a return to concealment, and the record's guards are three: the case against the recommended option at the first level and at full strength, which the scholastic form supplies; the clean-context review, whose counter-argument replaces the AI's own on the row and is written by a reader that did not draft it; and the periagogic movement, where the author's own account is taken before options are drafted at all. Whether those are enough is not settled by the literature, and the divergence is recorded as open rather than answered.
