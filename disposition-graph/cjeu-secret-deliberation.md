## Answer

Four articles do the whole of it. Article 2: "Before taking up his duties each
Judge shall, before the Court of Justice sitting in open court, take an oath to
perform his duties impartially and conscientiously and to preserve the secrecy of
the deliberations of the Court." Article 35: "The deliberations of the Court of
Justice shall be and shall remain secret." Article 36: "Judgments shall state the
reasons on which they are based. They shall contain the names of the Judges who
took part in the deliberations." And Article 20: "The oral procedure shall consist
of the hearing by the Court of agents, advisers and lawyers and of the submissions
of the Advocate General."

Article 36 is the division this node is deciding, written in one sentence. The
reasons go on the record and the names go on the record; who held what does not.
There is no dissent, no concurrence and no vote count, and the secrecy is neither a
convention nor a habit but a personal oath sworn in open court and a permanent
rule. What the tradition says this buys is a single institutional voice, a holding
that does not read as contingent on a bare majority, and protection for the
individual judge, which is acute here because a judge's mandate is renewable by the
very member state that may appear before them. What it loses is what its critics
and the Cour de cassation's own reform debate say it loses: a reader cannot tell a
unanimous holding from a split one, and cannot find the argument that nearly won.

The counter this puts to the recommendation is the sharpest in the survey, and it
is sharp because the tradition already does the part this record wants. It
publishes who deliberated. It then stops one step short, deliberately, on a stated
ground: a reader who can see who chose what reads the decision through the
personnel instead of through the reasons. Two of the five marks the recommendation
carries put exactly that above the argument, the author's own provisional leaning
and each named expert's choice. The tradition's claim, held under oath for seventy
years and inherited from a practice far older, is that a reader who sees that three
experts and the author's earlier self all chose option B will not then read the
case for option C, and that this is not a weak reader but the predictable effect of
the layout. Against a record whose stated purpose is to keep the author's explicit
intent rather than their impression, and whose own reading
`anchoring-and-adjustment` names the same mechanism, that is a serious charge and
not a stylistic one.

The compensating instrument is the part the record should take, and it is why this
reading produced an option rather than only an objection. The tradition does not
suppress the losing reasoning; it relocates it. The Advocate General's submissions
are separately delivered, individually attributed, published alongside the
judgment, and not binding, and they routinely differ from it. Divergent reasoning
lives in a signed companion text rather than as a mark on the holding. That is a
design this fact did not carry, and it is the shape of
`names-on-the-row-stances-in-a-companion`.

Where the borrowing is not clean, and the record says so rather than discovering it
later: the Advocate General is an office, with a duty to produce, and the
submissions exist whether or not anyone feels like writing them. A record that
takes the relocation without taking the office has moved the stances somewhere
nobody is obliged to write, and a relocation whose destination stays empty is a
suppression under another name. The reading is therefore evidence that the marks
have a cost the recommendation does not name, and evidence that the alternative has
a precondition the record has not met.
