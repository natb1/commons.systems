## Answer

The tradition that asks this node's question out loud — why keep a view that lost
— gives three different answers in three consecutive mishnayot, and then keeps all
three rather than choosing between them. At 1:4: "And why do they record the
opinions of Shammai and Hillel for naught? To teach the following generations that
a man should not [always] persist in his opinion, for behold, the fathers of the
world did not persist in their opinion." At 1:5: "And why do they record the
opinion of a single person among the many, when the halakhah must be according to
the opinion of the many? So that if a court prefers the opinion of the single
person it may depend on him. For no court may set aside the decision of another
court unless it is greater than it in wisdom and in number." And at 1:6, Rabbi
Judah dissenting from the reason itself: "If so, why do they record the opinion of
a single person among the many to set it aside? So that if a man shall say, Thus
have I received the tradition, it may be said to him, According to the [refuted]
opinion of that individual did you hear it."

The record takes three things. The first is attribution with the aggregation
withheld, and this is the strongest precedent the record has for the mark naming
an expert's choice. Every retained opinion is attributed by name — Shammai,
Hillel, a single person among the many — and the tradition never states a tally.
The operative rule is itself a count, the halakhah being according to the opinion
of the many, and the record still declines to write the number. That is the
arrangement the author's rule asks for, maintained for two millennia by a
tradition whose whole subject is what binds.

The second is the answer to how a chosen-but-not-final position is kept from being
read as final, and it is the opposite of hiding it. 1:6 states the exact failure
this record fears — a reader takes the retained non-binding view for the ruling
and says thus have I received the tradition — and the remedy is a mark on the face
of the record saying what the view is, not the view's removal. The defence against
misreading is the mark.

The third is the split between retaining and presenting. 1:4 through 1:6 are a
separate discussion of why to retain, and the reasons are given once, in their own
place, and never repeated beside each retained opinion.

The divergence is Rabbi Judah's, and his word should not be softened: the minority
is recorded in order to be set aside. On his reasoning the indispensable mark is
the one saying that a view does not act. Of the five marks the answer carries,
three say who chose an option and none says an option was passed over, the
recommended text saying the row carries five marks "and no others" — so the row
spends its budget on who chose rather than on what does not bind. What makes this
divergence worth recording rather than merely noting is where the omission came
from: the passed-over chip was on the row and the author cut it on 2026-09-04,
with the two other chips, when they cut the row's marks to three things. So this
tradition diverges from the author's own cut and not from an AI's choice, which is
the form in which it should reach them.
