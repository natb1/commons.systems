# Maintaining Alignment for Long-Horizon Agentic Workflows

Alignment, in AI research, is the observation that making a model smarter is only half of what it takes for it to create value. The other half is that the model has to want what you want — it has to be aligned with your true intentions. A more capable system pursuing the wrong objective is not a smaller problem than a less capable one; it is a larger one. And the more reasoning you delegate, the more that second half dominates. Past some point, maintaining alignment *is* the work.

> **The paperclip maximizer.** Nick Bostrom's thought experiment imagines an AI told to make paperclips that is good enough at the task to turn the planet into paperclips and the people into raw material — doing exactly what it was told and nothing you meant. The cartoon is about a superintelligence, but the lesson is mundane and immediate: the harm a system can do scales with its capability, and capability without a faithful model of your intent only points that power somewhere you didn't ask for. You don't need a superintelligence to feel this. You need an agent that runs long enough, unsupervised enough, to drift.

Frontier models are already capable enough that you can hand them a wide range of real work. State-of-the-art harnesses — the tools, the context management, the long-horizon execution strategies wrapped around the model — let them pursue an objective with broad capability and genuine autonomy. That part is largely solved, or solving. What's left for you is the part that doesn't come in the box: saying what you actually want, and keeping a running system pointed at it as it works. Your bottleneck is defining your intentions and maintaining alignment.

This is a field report on what that looks like in a system that runs every day. This repository operates an autonomous coding-agent workflow I call `dispatch`. I own it; it runs on my own GitHub, Firebase, and Anthropic accounts; and it plans, implements, reviews, and merges its own pull requests while I sleep. It's forkable, and you're reading the artifact it produces. The rest of this is a practical introduction to how it stays aligned over horizons long enough that drift is the default outcome, not the exception.

## Two queues, and one of them is the human

The machinery is two queues. The autonomous **dispatch queue** spends no human tokens; it grinds work forward tick by tick — plan, implement, fix checks, review, merge. The **office-hours queue** is its opposite: the only thing that de-escalates an item sitting there is a human prompt. Everything `dispatch` cannot resolve on its own — an ambiguous plan, a deviation from what was approved, a genuine judgment call — routes to office-hours and waits for me.

That second queue is the alignment apparatus. It's the one event in the loop the agent cannot fake: it cannot prompt itself out of office-hours. So the design question is never "how do I make the agent never need me." It's "how do I make sure the decisions that actually turn on my intent are the ones that reach me, and nothing else does." I stay in planning and design; the agent owns execution. My intent is never captured by the system, because the system is built to route every intent-shaped decision back to me.

The vocabulary the field has settled on maps onto this cleanly, so I'll use it. In [Birgitta Böckeler's terms](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/harness-engineering.html), the harness is **guides** plus **sensors**. The guides are the feedforward layer — the `.claude/rules/*.md` files and each skill's `SKILL.md`, read fresh before any phase acts; this is where my intentions live, as text the agent reads before it does anything. The sensors are the feedback layer, and they split in two: computational sensors are CI — lint, types, tests — reproduced locally so a red check is a fact, not an opinion; inferential sensors are an [LLM-as-judge review fan-out](https://www.langchain.com/blog/the-anatomy-of-an-agent-harness) that reads the diff and argues about it. Guides encode intent going in; sensors catch drift coming out. ([Mitchell Hashimoto's ratchet](https://mitchellh.com/writing/my-ai-adoption-journey) — every mistake becomes a rule the agent can't repeat — is how the guide layer grows.)

## Where alignment decays over a long horizon

The horizon keeps stretching. [METR's data has the reliably-completable task length doubling roughly every seven months](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/), which pushes the work into territory where agents do fail. Long-horizon failure isn't exotic — it's what alignment decay looks like in practice. Each named failure mode is a specific way a running agent's behavior drifts from what you intended, and the real content of a harness is where it puts each one when it happens.

| Failure mode (alignment decay) | What it looks like over a long horizon | Containing mechanism in dispatch |
|---|---|---|
| Reasoning drift | The agent's model of where things stand diverges from reality | State is re-derived from PR, CI, and labels every tick; there is no store behind the SCM to drift from |
| Context rot | Accumulated context degrades judgment | Context is cleared at the plan→implement boundary; a persisted `<!-- dispatch:plan -->` comment is the only carrier into a clean build |
| Premature victory | "Done" declared with nothing finished | Success requires a positive phase-completed marker; any abnormal exit with no marker fails safe to office-hours |
| Self-evaluation bias | The agent grades its own work generously | An adversarial-verify pass tasks severity-scaled skeptics with refuting a finding before any fix runs |
| Error compounding | One early mistake cascades | The per-phase context-clear plus re-derived state cap how far a single error can travel |
| Slop and verbosity creep | Redundant, eroding code accretes | The inferential review sensor plus a standing token-audit discipline |

The skeptic detail under self-evaluation bias is the one I trust most: the verifiers default to "refuted" under uncertainty, so a finding has to survive an attempt to kill it before any fix gets to act on it. That's alignment maintenance in miniature — the system assumes its own judgment is drifting and makes the judgment earn its place.

There's a live disagreement worth naming. [Anthropic's harness-design work](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps) argues you should keep stress-testing your scaffolding and strip out whatever stops being load-bearing as models improve — though it's careful that the space of useful harness shapes doesn't shrink, it *moves*. Against that, the reliability literature reads the degradation as intrinsic: [a reliability-science framework finds long-horizon success decaying faster than the per-step error rate would predict](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29231), and [SlopCodeBench finds code quality eroding across iterative tasks where human code stays flat](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.24755). I build `dispatch` as if degradation is intrinsic: it contains failure modes rather than betting a better model dissolves them. If a future model retires a mechanism, I'll remove it — but the default assumption is that the drift stays.

## Defining the intentions: `/align`

Containing drift is the maintenance half of alignment. The other half — the harder half — is defining the intentions in the first place, and refining them as the project and your own understanding move. That is the job of the skill this post is really an introduction to: `/align`.

`/align` is the entry point to the method, and the first thing a practitioner runs in a fork. Every run takes your current intent and the current state of the project and does two things: it re-aligns the next step to your declared intent, and it pushes execution ownership one level down where that pays — from you, to an AI skill, to a deterministic script. It works on two independent axes. **Decomposition** turns an intention into more concrete sub-intentions. **Delegation** reassigns who executes: human, AI, or procedure. They aren't the same axis — a concrete decision can stay yours because it's a matter of taste, while an abstract one can be handed safely to a skill. Each run happens at a higher altitude than the last, so the system you own grows the way your understanding does.

That loop is what makes the rest self-correcting. The failure-mode containment above keeps a running agent from drifting away from a fixed target; `/align` is how the target itself stays true as you learn what you actually want. Maintaining alignment is both jobs at once: catch the drift of a running agent, and keep redefining the intent precisely enough that "aligned" means something.

None of the individual patterns here is an invention; the distinctiveness is the integration and its direction. [OpenAI's account of leaning into an agent-first codebase](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/) and the broader [collected practice](https://github.com/walkinglabs/awesome-harness-engineering) cover most of the parts. Two nearby projects show the seams: Code Conductor runs a fully autonomous loop with no in-loop human-escalation path — its `conductor:blocked` label is defined but never set by any agent — while Claude Plan Orchestrator keeps human approval gates but commits a derived status mirror of an authoritative SQLite store. `dispatch` has no authoritative store behind the SCM at all: the PR, CI, and label ground truth *is* the state, re-derived every tick, with office-hours as the peer queue where the human is the one event the loop can't fake.

A harness built to be left is one whose success is your eventual independence from it — the inverse of a retention metric — and one that keeps your intent in your hands the whole way. It's all here, and it's forkable. Take the parts that serve you and leave the rest.
