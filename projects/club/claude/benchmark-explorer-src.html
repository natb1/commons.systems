Reading the model

Why café ≠ walk-ins + catering as one tx/day number

Walk-ins and catering are different generative processes with different units and different levers, so the model sums their contributions, not their transaction counts. Walk-ins come from foot traffic and site quality: tx/day × ticket × 360 × the café margin. Catering comes from the rooms engine: (club sessions × club attach + events × event attach) × ~$70 order × 52 × ~60%. Folding catering into tx/day would price a ~$70 pre-order at the $8.25 counter ticket and would hide the real coupling — catering rises when the rooms levers move, which the matrix now reflects row by row. At plan marks it reproduces the plan exactly: ~1.7 orders/wk ≈ $6K revenue, ~$3.7K margin (D15's "modest but real"). Its ceiling is honest too: even at the utilization ceiling with generous attach it tops out around ~$13K of margin — an attach that tracks the rooms, not a stream.

The “SN/HT: subtract ~$22K” confusion, resolved

That sentence in the matrix doc bundles two separate site differences into one number. Decomposed at the base row (33% utilization):

Little Italy at its 100 tx/day mark−$13K
SN/HT cost basis at equal traffic (occupancy $64K vs $78K)+$14K
SN/HT expected traffic: 20 fewer walk-ins/day (×~$1.8K)−$36K
SN/HT at its own 80 tx/day mark−$35K

So “−$22K per cell” is the net of both (−13 → −35), valid only when comparing each site at its own expected column. Applied literally to a matrix whose columns are absolute tx/day, it double-counts the traffic difference (SN/HT at 80 tx would come out −$71K, contradicting §6). Yes — the levers resolve it: here the Location lever moves only the cost basis (+$14K in SN/HT's favor), and traffic is whatever column you read; the two effects can no longer be conflated. SN/HT's real story is cheaper floor, weaker expected café column — which is why it still only works toward the bottom-right.

Wage rungs (the one ordinal lever)

Labor scales from the plan's grid (~100 staffed hr/wk ≈ 5,200 hr/yr — the plan's $120K line at its $23/hr). Each rung is now priced bottom-up from three parts, which the rail shows for whichever rung is selected: the worker's take-home (cash wage, before their own taxes), the employer's payroll load on it (FICA 7.65% + FUTA/MD unemployment ~0.7% on capped bases + workers' comp ~2.0% ≈ 10.35%), and employer-paid benefits. Total comp per hour is the sum — it is what the labor line spends, not an asserted round number.

What the bottom-up pricing corrected. The old MIT rung was carried “loaded-light” at $21.50/hr — barely 2% above the $21.03 it claimed to pay, which is less than the employer's payroll taxes alone. Priced properly it is $23.21, and the labor line it implies is ~$121K rather than ~$112K. The same arithmetic run backwards on D6 is the sharper finding: $23/hr “fully loaded” buys $20.84 of take-home — under MIT's living wage, not over it. So D6 as written is not a living-wage commitment plus anything; “Living+” is only true of the rung once it is priced at $24.71. The site basis is still calibrated on the plan's own $120K labor line, so the default view now sits ~$1K under the published §6 base case for that reason alone.

Location moves the expectations, not just the costs

Switching site re-anchors three things at once: the cost basis (the §6 calibration), the café columns (drawn at 70–130% of that site's mark, so "top decile" is corridor-relative rather than a Little Italy number applied everywhere), and the walk-in default (snapped to the site's mark, since that is the base-case expectation the counts test against). What stays absolute is the 70/day lean stop — which is the quiet warning in SN/HT's column headers: its expected traffic sits barely above its own stop rule.

What the owner's draw has to clear

The old $30K line is gone from this tool. It was the plan's partial-income floor — the trigger test of §9 (debt service + ~$30K), premised on other household income covering the rest. That is a household-survival number, not a measure of when the business is worth the owner's labor and capital. The standard test for the latter is economic profit: owner comp must cover (a) a market or living wage for the hours actually worked and (b) a required return on the equity at risk. Small-business valuation formalizes the same idea as SDE minus a fair replacement salary. Two tiers replace the one line.

The wage half. The owner's gridded week is ~40 hr (~30 on the floor + ~10 admin and sales), and it is additional to the ~100 staffed hr/wk the labor line buys — the plan's grid is already net of the owner's floor hours (D5/D6), so nothing here is counted twice. Those hours are priced at the Wages rung, but not at the full staff rate: an owner drawing from the LLC carries the SE tax (15.3% on 92.35% of the draw, which makes a draw of cash × 1.0755 the equal of a wage of cash — so the employer FICA half, 7.65%, is the right uplift), and buys their own benefits, but unemployment insurance and workers' comp cover employees, not the member-owner. So the owner's hour is take-home + FICA + benefits: $21.03 × 1.0765 = $22.64 at MIT living, against the $23.21 the same hour costs as staff. That is 40 × 52 × $22.64 ≈ $47.1K. It moves with the Wages lever, and that coupling is the point: an owner who pays the $16.55 market floor is pricing their own year at $34K, not $47K. But a living wage on its own is not a bar — it says nothing about the money. Both tiers below start from it and add a return.

1 — Living wage & zero opportunity cost. The wage plus 10% on the capital at risk: what that cash would earn sitting passively somewhere else. Clear this and the owner is no worse off than they'd be taking a job and leaving the money in the market — the venture has cost them nothing but risk. Capital at risk is its own lever, defaulted to the midpoint of the plan's grant-free cash-to-open range ($180–300K). At $240K the bar is $47.1K + $24K = $71.1K.

2 — The economic band. The wage plus 20% or more — the risk-adjusted rate a buyer of an illiquid, single-operator small business would demand. This is where the venture earns genuine economic profit rather than merely breaking even against the alternatives. At $240K it opens at $95.1K. On the SBLP-buy path the ~$10K/yr of principal paydown counts toward the return side, so both tiers are tested against draw + equity build.

Where the tiers land on this model. The gate-clearing case (~110 walk-ins/day at ~55% utilization) reaches +$29K, and the wage alone would take roughly ~10 more walk-ins/day on top of that. The bar — wage plus zero opportunity cost — needs about ~23 more walk-ins/day than the gate case, or ~11 more filled weekly sessions, which puts it past the café's top decile and above the room evidence ceiling. The economic band opens at the top of the plan's own "Strong" scenario ($85–95K). Read plainly: on the plan's own numbers this venture pays its owner a living wage long before it pays for the capital, and the derived gates say so in red.

The gates are outputs, not fixtures

The $0 line is fixed by arithmetic, and the bar by the Wages rung plus the capital lever, but where they fall on the grid depends on every lever outside the two engines — pay market wages and the break-even roster shrinks (and the bar drops with it); take the SN/HT floor and it shrinks again while the café mark weakens. The "derived gates" strip computes, from the current site, deal, and constraint settings: the utilization and club roster needed for $0 and for the bar at the site's café mark, the walk-in count needed at gate-case rooms (55%), and the G2 cash test. A gate shown in red is one that current settings push outside the evidence region (or the cash cap) — meaning those settings demand proof the pilot can't currently supply.

Capital & concessions (the G2 half of the deal)

Build-out, TI allowance, rent abatement, and grants don't touch stabilized Year-2 owner comp — they decide whether the venture can be reached with the available cash (D7: <$150K). Cash at risk ≈ (build-out − TI − grants) + working-capital reserve (~$50–65K, floored — abatement months relieve the Year-1 ramp portion at the site's monthly occupancy). If lease-path all-in can't fit the cap after TI negotiation, that is a G2 stop, not a stretch (risk 4). On the SBLP-buy path the cash math changes kind: 10% contribution + closing + FF&E exceeds own cash by design, so the gate becomes "executed community-development terms in hand" (D8).

Two different “bail” lines — one static, one derived

The plan has two stop rules that are easy to conflate. The lean stop at 70 walk-ins/day is a pre-signature evidence rule (risk 1): if counts come in under 70 post-haircut in both markets, no lease gets signed. It is deliberately static and absolute — it is a precommitment about what evidence justifies risking capital at all, not a statement about operating survival. The bail line is the operating question — how deep a loss the funded venture can sustain — and that is not static: it is the reserve spread over the decision horizon, −(venture cash − net build-out + abatement relief) × 12 / runway. Every capital lever moves it: harder TI negotiation deepens it, a build-out overrun shallows it. Cells red-ringed in the matrix burn the reserve before the verdict date — losses there don't buy information, they just end the venture. At plan defaults ($150K − $85K = $65K reserve over 24 months) the line sits at ≈ −$32K/yr: which is exactly why §6 calls the Lean scenario (−$65 to −$95K) “not survivable” while the base case (−$13K) is a slow bleed the reserve can carry past an honest verdict (~5 years of runway).

What changed across versions, and what deliberately didn't

Adopted: catering endogenous to the rooms engine; wages ordinal; site-relative café columns and re-anchored defaults; derived gates; the capital/concessions submodel; club churn reclassified into the market hypothesis (it is a claim about group retention behavior — the pilot measures it — not a property of the site or deal); room count fixed at 3 (a 4th room without footprint cost was a free-lunch lever); the $30K partial-income floor retired in favor of two economic-profit tiers — a living wage at zero opportunity cost, then the economic band at a risk-adjusted return — with capital at risk as a lever; wage rungs re-priced bottom-up from take-home + payroll + benefits, with MIT living as both the default and the rung the others are compared against, and D6 renamed Living+; the owner's wage-equivalent separated from the staff rate, carrying FICA and benefits but not the unemployment and workers'-comp lines that cover employees only; and the back-solved site basis retired in favor of a line per §5/§6 quantity — the café contribution margin promoted to a lever, the day-room and print lines modelled, and revenue-variable operations (card fees + marketing) broken out from the fixed operating base, which is what reconciles the plan's own $1.8K marginal with its own 68% pro-forma margin. Revenue-variable operations had been declined in an earlier version on comparability grounds; reconciling the plan with itself is worth more than cell-for-cell comparability with a matrix built on the error.

Considered and declined, to keep descriptive power without false precision: (1) a price–utilization demand curve — the only elasticity evidence is the analog's near-sellout at $5–10/hr, one point; inventing a curve would launder a guess into a surface, so price and utilization stay independent with the evidence region shaded instead — the Phase 0.5 graduation ask is precisely the experiment that buys this curve. (2) Seasonality — the plan handles it as a 20–25% haircut on counts before they enter, not as a model term.

Every line is its own quantity — there is no catch-all

An earlier version carried a single site basis of $107K, back-solved so that plan marks reproduced §6. That number did not survive a sniff test, and it should not have: §6's occupancy plus operations for Little Italy is $143K, not $107K. The $36K difference was two things the label never said — the day-room and print lines the tool did not model (worth ~$14K of contribution), and a $22K café-margin error. The old café margin, 60.6%, was reverse-engineered from the plan's stated marginal of ~$1.8K per walk-in/day (8.25 × 360 × 0.606 = 1,800) and then used to compute a level. A marginal rate is not an average rate; the gap went into the plug.

Both of the plan's numbers were right. §6's $104K of COGS on a $297K café is a 32% cost of sale — a 68% contribution margin, and that is a gross figure. The $1.8K marginal is net of the operating costs that ride on revenue. Break those out and the two reconcile: 8.25 × 360 × (68% − 5.1%) = $1.87K. So the café margin is now a lever in the market hypothesis, defaulted to 68%, and the operating costs are split where §6 itself splits them.

How the operations split is derived, not assumed. §6 gives one operations figure per site — $65K in Little Italy, $62K in SN/HT. The two sites differ in revenue and in nothing else about operations, so the pair solves for a revenue-proportional part and a fixed base. It comes out at 5.1% of gross revenue (card fees ~3% + marketing ~2.5%, exactly the bottom-up lines §6 names) and a fixed base of $46.8K (utilities, insurance, software, R&M). The check is that the fixed base falls out identical at both sites — it is solved from Little Italy and lands on SN/HT to the decimal. That is what tells you the split is real and not fitted.
