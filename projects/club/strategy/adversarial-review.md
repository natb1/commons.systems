# Adversarial Review — Albemarle Tea & Odd Prints
**Against plan v0.2.1, open-questions, entity-structure, and schedule (all Aug 27, 2026)**

> **STATUS: RESOLVED (Aug 27, 2026, evening).** The operator reviewed these findings, largely endorsed the steelman, and resolved every flagged tension by interview — decisions D1–D12 recorded in `business-plan.md` §0 (v0.3). All four planning docs were rewritten to match: rooms-led reframe, commons free-and-limited, corrected pro forma at living wage, lease-path sizing, dual G1 gate with precommitted deposit-backed bars, two-arm trigger test. This document is kept as the record of the critique; the plan is the record of the resolutions.
*Role: hostile reviewer. The job here is to break the plan, not to be fair to it. Where the plan already names a risk, it gets attacked only if the mitigation itself is weak. Findings are ordered by how much damage they do, and each ends with the cheapest way to test or fix it.*

---

## Part 0 — The concept itself

*Before the numbers: is the idea sound? Findings here are labeled C1–C5.*

### C1 (STRUCTURAL): This is five marginal businesses wearing one coat, and the complexity is superadditive while the revenue is subadditive

Café, co-working operation, private event venue, bookstore-with-lending-library, gallery — plus printing and a food market on deck. Each of these is, individually, a thin-margin business that fails often. The bet is that bundling fixes that: shared rent, shared staff, daypart utilization of the same rooms. The daypart-utilization piece is real — it's the one genuine synergy, and it's a real-estate synergy (see Finding 4). But the costs compound faster than the sharing: five sets of operational skills, three licensing regimes, mixed-occupancy insurance, inventory in two retail categories, and five distinct marketing channels — all landing on one operator (Finding 17). The graveyard of bookstore-cafés and board-game cafés is full of concepts that discovered customers use the cheapest facet of a hybrid and staff must be competent at all of them. A bundle wins only when the *same customer* values several facets at once and switching providers is costly. Who is that customer here? The plan never names one. The person who wants all five facets is, so far, the founder.

### C2 (STRUCTURAL): The commons paradox — the concept's mission success and business success are the same variable with opposite signs

The aspiration is a room where you can sit without buying. Every hour the space succeeds *as a commons* — full of people sitting, browsing, borrowing — it is failing as a café and cannibalizing its own paid products (Finding 5). Every intervention that improves the economics (paid boundaries, more private events displacing open hours, trimming café hours per risk #1) erodes the commons. The plan's own resolution of this tension is the Phase 2 nonprofit arm — which is a quiet admission that **the commons layer is not a business and never was.** Said plainly: the commercially evaluable concept is "bookable rooms plus a café"; the library-like layer is philanthropy attached to it. That's a perfectly honorable structure — the plan half-knows it — but the concept should be pitched, sized, and gate-tested as the former, with the commons costed as a donation the LLC chooses to make, not blended into the revenue story.

### C3 (HIGH): The concept is shaped by what the founder wants to exist, not by a named customer's unmet need

Nowhere in four planning documents is there a specific customer with a specific, painful, unserved need. There are personas by implication — the solo professional, the RPG group, the browsing neighbor — but every one of them is currently served somewhere: Pratt branches lend books and host meeting rooms for free, Baltimore has a co-working and sublease glut (Finding 14), Peerspace lists small venues by the page, game stores give tables away to sell product, and Station North / Creative Alliance already run the arts programming. The demand-test instruments (#4, #5) are exactly right — and the telling detail is that the services were *confirmed* on Aug 27, before any of those tests returned data. The concept was ratified on mission resonance, then sent out to find its customers. The honest sequencing is the reverse, and the plan's own gates still allow it: treat the confirmations as provisional until the deposit-backed versions of #4/#5 come home.

### C4 (MEDIUM): Tea is a destination product being asked to do a habit product's job

The café co-anchor is modeled at 80–100 transactions a day, 360 days a year — that is *habit* traffic, and the habit beverage is coffee. Tea skews occasional, contemplative, destination — on-brand for the commons, wrong-shaped for the transaction counts the pro forma leans on. If the counter quietly becomes a full coffee program to hit the numbers (as it will), fine — but then say so and cost it; if it stays tea-forward, haircut the tx/day assumptions before comparing markets on them.

### C5 (HIGH): Every comparable that works is subsidized — the plan cites one as proof and skips the punchline

The plan invokes Creative Alliance as proof the arts-venue-plus-community model works in Highlandtown. Creative Alliance is a 501(c)(3) running on grants, donations, and memberships. The free-rooms competitor is the tax-funded library system. The co-working comparables that survive at neighborhood scale are usually landlord-subsidized amenities. The nearest working instances of this concept are all structures where someone other than the customer pays for the commons. That doesn't kill the idea — it locates it: the §9 trigger test ("if it can't cover debt service + $30K without permanent grants, it's factually a charity") may be less a safety valve than a forecast. The concept as stated is most likely viable in exactly two forms: **(a)** a lean rooms-and-café LLC that *owns its building on subsidized debt* and donates a commons layer it can afford, or **(b)** a nonprofit from the start, funded as what it is. The current plan is engineered to discover which — that's its real strength — but the review's read of the evidence is that (a) is the only version that preserves the operator's equity, and it survives only if Part I's corrected numbers still clear the floor.

### The steelman, so the target is fair

There is a version of this concept that deserves to exist and can work: a neighborhood with real residential density; the building owned, not leased, on cheap long-term debt; the night sold first — four to six deposit-paying recurring groups and a standing event pipeline as the anchor tenant — with the café as daytime amenity and front door; books and commons hours as explicit, budgeted philanthropy. That version is a modest real-estate-and-hospitality business with a mission attached, and Part I's findings are the distance between it and the current pro forma. What does not survive adversarial review is the version implied by the current framing: a café-led commons whose margins carry everything else.

## Part I — The economics as modeled

### Finding 1 (CRITICAL): The operations line is roughly half of reality, and the shortfall comes straight out of owner comp

The pro forma carries **$38K of "operations"** against $370–429K of revenue — 8.9% — for a 2,800–3,500 sq ft food-service building running a ~14-hour day, 360 days a year. Walk the components: card processing alone at a blended ~2.7% is **~$11–12K**; utilities for a food-service space on that operating schedule, **$12–18K**; insurance for a café-plus-assembly-plus-private-events program with BYO alcohol exposure (the plan's own #20 flags this), **$10–20K**; POS, booking, and access-control software, **$3–6K**; repairs, trash, pest, internet, accounting, licenses, **$10–15K**; marketing, currently **$0** (Finding 15). A defensible bottom-up line is **$60–85K**, before surprises in a 150-year-old building.

Every dollar of understatement is a dollar of owner comp, because owner comp is the residual. At a $65–80K ops line, the scenario table rewrites itself:

| Scenario | Plan's owner comp | At realistic ops ($65–80K) |
|---|---|---|
| Little Italy lease | ~$73K | **~$31–46K** |
| Little Italy buy | ~$68K | **~$26–41K** |
| SN/HT buy | ~$54K | **~$12–27K** |
| SN/HT lease | ~$37K | **~ −$5K to +$10K** |

Against the plan's own ~$25–35K partial-income floor, only the Little Italy scenarios clear reliably. **The mission-fit markets — the ones v0.2.1 just re-weighted toward — fail or graze zero.** The partial-income adjustment didn't widen the set of workable sites; an understated cost line did.

*Fix: rebuild the ops line bottom-up (real insurance quotes, real utility history from listing agents, card fees as a % of revenue) before G1, and re-run the market comparison. This is a spreadsheet afternoon and it likely reverses the plan's newest strategic conclusion.*

### Finding 2 (CRITICAL): There is no working capital, no Year-1 ramp, and the cash target quietly depends on winning grants

The revenue table is labeled **"stabilized Year 2."** Nothing anywhere funds Year 1. A café-and-membership business plausibly runs at 50–70% of stabilized revenue in its first year — call it a **$40–80K cumulative shortfall** — and the Lean scenario the plan itself models burns **−$56K/yr** at full staffing. Yet the "cash to close/open" ranges ($80–205K) contain fit-out and closing costs only: no operating reserve, no ramp funding, no personal-runway line for an operator drawing little in the first months.

Worse, footnote ² gets the lease scenarios "under the $150K target" only *"after landlord TI allowance, façade grant, and a Business Boost grant if won."* Section 4b's stated principle is **"model the base case with no grants; treat awards as acceleration."** The pro forma honors that rule; the cash-to-open math violates it. Applied consistently, realistic cash to open is **$180–300K** — meaning the Albemarle house (#21, deferred to Weeks 5–6) is not a side question. It is probably the funding plan, and the plan should say so out loud.

*Fix: add a working-capital + Year-1 ramp line to every scenario; restate cash-to-open grant-free; move the Albemarle keep/sell/hold decision ahead of G1, since it determines which scenarios are fundable at all.*

### Finding 3 (HIGH): Three FTE cannot cover this operating model, so the plan is buying the owner's labor at a discount the plan never discloses

The building is open roughly 8 AM–10 PM, 360 days: **~98 front-of-house hours a week minimum at single coverage.** Three FTE is ~120 paid hours. That leaves ~22 hours a week for *all* second-person coverage — morning rush, event setup and hosting, day-to-night room turnover, cleaning, receiving, bookkeeping, and the marketing nobody budgeted. The remainder is the owner. The realistic owner load is **50–60+ hours a week** against a ~$30K comp target — under $12/hour for the person carrying keys, closing duty, and all management.

The wage level has the same problem: $126K ÷ 3 FTE = $42K fully loaded ≈ **~$18/hour gross** after employer burden. The plan's stated aspiration is "pay fair wages"; living-wage estimates for a single adult in Baltimore run well above $20/hour. Funding the aspiration costs another **$25–40K/yr** — which, stacked on Finding 1, puts even the Little Italy scenarios near the floor.

*Fix: build an actual staffing grid (hours × role × day) instead of an FTE count; price it at the wage the plan is willing to defend in public; and state the owner's weekly hours as a line item. If the honest number is 55 hours for $30K, write that sentence down and see if it survives being read.*

### Finding 4 (HIGH): The subsidy thesis is inverted — the rooms subsidize the café, not the other way around

The plan's stated engine: *"the higher-margin public service (the café) subsidizes the lower-margin community services."* Run contribution margins on the plan's own numbers. The café: $297K revenue − $98K COGS − the large majority of the labor load (it's the reason anyone staffs a 14-hour counter) ≈ **~$110K contribution on ~$126K of labor consumed**. The room program: $120K revenue (co-working + evenings) with near-zero COGS and modest labor ≈ **~$100K+ contribution**. Per revenue dollar and per labor hour, **the rooms are the high-margin business; independent cafés net 2.5–7% in a good year.** The plan even confesses this in risk #1's mitigation: *"trim public café hours before trimming rental availability"* — you don't trim the subsidizer first.

This isn't wordplay; it misdirects the biggest decision in the plan. The entire modeled gap between markets ($60K) is café throughput, so G1 is being decided on the *lowest-margin* stream, while the plan hand-waves the rental streams as "roughly location-neutral" (see Finding 7). The G1 comparison should be weighted toward room demand and evening-corridor viability, with café counts as a secondary check.

*Fix: reframe — the café is the amenity and the draw; the rooms and the building are the business. Then re-ask which market wins.*

### Finding 5 (HIGH): "Sit without buying" is at war with the day-pass and membership lines

The brand promise — a library-like room where you can sit without buying — is also a **free substitute for the $25 day pass** and for part of the $150 membership. Enforce a paying boundary and the civic-room story dies; don't enforce it and the ~$18K/yr day-pass line is fiction and membership value collapses to bookable-room access. Free competitors sharpen the problem: Pratt branches rent meeting rooms at no charge, game stores host free play space to sell product, and churches and community centers underprice everything. The recurring-club revenue ($800/mo) is modest enough to be believable; the 60 day passes/month are not, in a building whose signature promise is that you don't have to pay to be there.

*Fix: define now, in writing, what is free and what is paid (e.g., commons floor free always; bookable rooms, offices, and reserved desks paid), price the membership on room access + perks rather than seat access, and delete or heavily haircut the day-pass line.*

### Finding 6 (MEDIUM): The evening stream is priced off listing rates, sold by nobody, in a supply glut

10 private events/month at $350 is ~2.3 bookings every weekend, sustained all year, in a city where Peerspace and Giggster carry pages of venues at $38–150/hr — listing prices, not achieved prices, from hosts competing for the same small-gathering demand. Events also consume labor the 3-FTE model doesn't hold (Finding 3) and marketing the budget doesn't hold (Finding 15). And the plan calls rental streams "roughly location-neutral" while risk #4 admits evening safety perception can suppress exactly this daypart in Station North and Bromo — both claims can't be true. Private-event customers pick venues on parking, perceived safety, and ambience; the evening stream is *more* location-sensitive than the café, not less.

*Fix: treat 10/mo as the Strong case, ~5–6/mo as Base; make the pre-commitment test (#5) deposit-backed recurring groups, not conversations; and strike "location-neutral" from the plan.*

### Finding 7 (LOW): The retail line isn't a revenue stream, and books were confirmed before they were modeled

$12K of retail at 60% COGS is **$4.8K/yr of gross margin — $400 a month** — against inventory cash, shelf square footage, curation time, and shrinkage. The lending shelf then cannibalizes the sales it sits next to. That's fine *as mission*, and the plan half-says so, but it also promoted books to "confirmed service" on Aug 27 while explicitly deferring the modeling to v0.3 — a commitment made ahead of its analysis. Small dollars, bad habit.

*Fix: model books as programming cost with incidental revenue, and let #6b's design work precede any floor-plan commitment at G2.*

## Part II — Financing and structure

### Finding 8 (CRITICAL): The plan's expected path is its weakest scenario; the good scenario is a lottery ticket being treated as a plan

Chain the plan's own rules. (i) Buy only with SBLP-grade debt; market debt means lease (§6). (ii) SBLP is a **brand-new program**: one $14M first round statewide, unknown cadence, unverified rate and term — the modeled 4%/30yr is explicitly NBW-era nostalgia. A first-time operator with no operating history is applying into an oversubscribed inaugural pool. (iii) Therefore the *modal* outcome is the lease path — which pays $37K in the mission market on the plan's own numbers, and ~$0 after Finding 1. Even a successful SBLP award at, say, 6%/20yr instead of 4%/30yr adds **~$17K/yr** of debt service to the SN/HT buy case ($33.5K → ~$50K), taking its corrected owner comp below the floor.

The plan says the right sentence ("do not close a purchase on bridge/market debt with SBLP 'expected'") and then spends most of its strategic weight on the buy case anyway. A plan must be survivable on its modal path, not its best one.

*Fix: size everything to the no-SBLP lease path with purchase option (#17 already points there); treat SBLP as the acceleration event, exactly as the plan already treats grants. If the corrected lease path can't clear the ~$30K floor anywhere, that is a G1 stop, not a financing detail.*

### Finding 9 (HIGH): The downside is personally guaranteed debt on an illiquid special-purpose building — and the plan never prices its own failure

Community-development and SBA lenders alike will almost certainly require a **personal guarantee**. If the concept fails in year 2, the exit is selling a rehabbed, special-purpose building in a transitional corridor — and the market has already told you what it thinks of that collateral: 817 N. Howard is offered at an **8.24% cap**, a price that screams demanded risk premium. Meanwhile #21 contemplates selling the Albemarle house to fund the 10% contribution, which re-couples the operator's housing to the venture through the balance sheet after v0.2 proudly decoupled it through the floor plan. The risk register has burnout and financing risk; it has no line for "what do I personally owe, on what asset, if this doesn't work."

*Fix: add a failure-cost paragraph to §8: guarantee exposure, realistic fire-sale value per market, and which personal assets are pledged. Decide the Albemarle question with that paragraph in view.*

### Finding 10 (HIGH): The plan has ratcheted — fixed costs up, personal bar down, concept untouched — and every gate is self-scored

Across three revisions the breakeven floor rose from ~$250K to ~$305–315K while the owner-comp bar fell from ~$65K to ~$30K. Each time the plan hit a wall (ADA, thin margins), the concept survived by the operator agreeing to accept less and commit more. That is the exact signature of escalation of commitment, executed by an unusually self-aware person — the trigger test in §9 is genuinely good, but it is administered by the person who wants to pass it, scored against counts that person collected, on a bar that person just lowered. And the demand tests count "soft commitments" from the operator's own network, which convert at maybe 10–30%.

*Fix: precommit G1 pass/fail numbers now, in writing, before the counts finish (the 70-tx/day stop rule is a good start — extend it to room demand and corrected owner comp); make demand-test currency refundable deposits, not conversations; and recruit one disinterested outsider — not a friend of the project — to red-team the G1 memo against the precommitted numbers.*

### Finding 11 (MEDIUM): The tax-credit assumption is probably overstated in exactly the years it's needed

The SN/HT buy case nets ~$10K/yr of property-tax credit into occupancy. But the A&E credit applies to the **increased assessment from renovation** of space used by a qualifying arts enterprise, and Enterprise Zone credits run on **incremental** assessed value — if the purchase sets the base and the rehab is modest, the credit base is small in years 1–5, which is when the coverage math is tightest. Certification, filings, and the 90-day windows are all execution risk on top. The plan flags "verify, don't assume" (#14) and then assumes anyway, inside the headline pro forma.

*Fix: compute the credit from an actual assessment scenario for each finalist building; carry $0 in the base pro forma and the computed figure in a sensitivity row.*

## Part III — Building and regulatory

### Finding 12 (HIGH): Assembly occupancy is v0.1's killer wearing a new coat, and the plan's tripwire is set too low

v0.1 died because a building couldn't legally hold the concept. The same class of risk is alive in v0.2: recurring evening gatherings can flip rooms from B/M to **A-2/A-3 assembly**, and change-of-occupancy on 1870s–1900s Baltimore stock routinely triggers **sprinklers, egress, and restroom upgrades** that run well past the schedule's "architect flags >$75K → widen search" threshold — a sprinkler retrofit alone can eat that. The plan's #9 asks the right question; the schedule's tripwire understates the likely answer for nearly every candidate on the list.

Worse, the mitigations collide: risk #1's fix for the labor problem is **keyed, self-service after-hours access** for gaming groups — i.e., an *unstaffed* assembly use with BYO alcohol, which is precisely what fire code and the insurance underwriter (#20) will refuse. The labor fix creates a code-and-coverage problem the plan hasn't noticed it created.

*Fix: get the occupancy-classification read and a sprinkler-status check on every candidate at the architect walkthrough (already scheduled — add sprinklers explicitly); raise the walk-away threshold honestly; and price the unstaffed-access model with an insurance broker before it appears in any pro forma as a labor saving.*

### Finding 13 (MEDIUM): No capital reserve on 150-year-old buildings

Every acquisition candidate predates 1900. None of the pro formas carry a maintenance/capex reserve — roof, masonry, ancient water service, HVAC sized for a day/night split. A lender will impose one; reality will impose a bigger one. Call it $8–12K/yr, which — refrain — comes out of owner comp.

*Fix: add a reserve line to the buy cases now; ask each listing agent for capital-work history and fold the architect's systems read (#12) into a 10-year capex sketch.*

## Part IV — Market and process

### Finding 14 (MEDIUM): Selling co-working into a record office glut

Baltimore's office vacancy has been setting record highs, which floods the market with cheap sublease space, landlord-run flex suites, and desperate Class B landlords — all competing for the same solo professionals and two-person firms the plan wants at $150/desk and $600/office. The residential "work near home" thesis is the defensible niche, but it's exactly the segment the free-seating problem (Finding 5) undercuts. The absolute numbers are modest, which is the plan's best defense — but the 20-member base still has to be *found and kept* (churn on small-space memberships runs several percent a month, so the plan must recruit roughly half a member base per year just to stand still).

*Fix: the pre-commitment test (#4) is the right instrument — run it with deposits, and count only strangers, not friends.*

### Finding 15 (MEDIUM): There is no marketing budget, no acquisition plan, and no one with time to execute one

Stabilized Year 2 assumes 20 members, 60 day passes, 10 events, and 4–6 recurring groups continuously replenished. The pro forma allocates $0 to making that happen, and Findings 3 and 6 show nobody has hours to do it unpaid. Community-relations-first is a posture, not a funnel.

*Fix: 2–3% of revenue as a marketing line ($8–13K), plus named owner hours for it in the staffing grid.*

### Finding 16 (MEDIUM): The counts that decide G1 are peak-season counts, on a three-week clock

G1 lands ~Sep 21 on two weeks of late-August/September data — the best café weather of the year, Station North inflated by MICA's semester start, Little Italy in festival season. February exists. A market chosen on September data can be 25% smaller in the trough, and the schedule's compressed Phase 0a leaves no time to find that out before an LOI.

*Fix: haircut all counts 20–25% before comparing to the 80/100 tx/day bar, and put one winter validation weekend before G3 (contract), where there's still a contingency to exercise.*

### Finding 17 (HIGH): The plan never says where the operator's time comes from

"Partial operator income — other income covers the rest" is doing enormous quiet work. If the other income is a job, the 50–60-hour owner-operator load in Finding 3 is impossible and the whole labor model collapses. If it's passive or household income, the plan should say so and model the household's actual cash flow, because that household is also the guarantor (Finding 9) and possibly the source of the 10% contribution (#21). A one-person keyed operation also has no answer for the week the operator is sick.

*Fix: one honest paragraph in v0.3: source and reliability of the other income, the operator's weekly hour budget, and the named-keyholder plan for absence.*

## What the plan gets right (so this review is aimed correctly)

This is an unusually honest planning document: the no-grants base case, counts-before-contract, the pre-contract architect screen that institutionalizes the v0.1 lesson, the refusal to close on bridge debt, the SBA fallback term sheet, the 70-tx/day stop rule, and a written trigger test for the nonprofit question are all things most first-time operators never write down. The findings above are almost all places where the plan's own principles — model without grants, validate before committing, survive the modal path — were stated and then not applied to one more layer of the numbers.

## Verdict

**On the concept:** it is a real-estate-and-rooms business mislabeled as a café-led commons, ratified before its demand tests returned, whose working comparables are all subsidized. It survives review only in the steelman form (Part 0): building owned on cheap debt, night program sold first, café as amenity, commons as budgeted philanthropy. In any other form, the §9 trigger test is likely to fire — and the plan deserves credit for having written the test that will catch it.

**On the plan:** the current pro forma does not survive: the ops line understates costs by roughly $25–45K/yr, working capital and Year 1 are unfunded, the labor model runs on undisclosed owner hours, and the expected financing path (lease, no SBLP) fails the plan's own ~$30K floor in the mission-preferred market. The strategic thesis is also mislabeled — this is a **rooms-and-building business with a café-shaped front door**, and G1 should be decided on that basis. None of this says stop. It says: rebuild the cost base bottom-up, fund Year 1, precommit the gate numbers, and re-run the market comparison — all before Sep 21, all cheaper than any mistake they prevent. If the corrected numbers clear the floor only in the SBLP-buy case, then the honest conclusion under the plan's own trigger logic is that this commons is viable only on subsidized debt — which is still a fundable story, but it should be pursued as that story, not discovered as one.
