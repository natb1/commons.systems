## 1. What the financing landscape actually says (verified Aug 2026)

- **Neighborhood BusinessWorks was renamed/reimagined in July 2026 as the DHCD Small Business Lending Program (SBLP).** The Aug 17 – Sep 17, 2026 round is the *first* competitive round of the new program ($14M in Small Business Direct Loans). This explains the conflicting cadence references: older NBW material described a different rhythm; the new program's round cadence **has not been announced** — it is a consultation question, not a research question.
- **Structure does not gate the loan.** Eligible applicants must be "organized as a for-profit **or nonprofit** entity," in a Sustainable Community, engaged in commercial activity. So the entity decision can be made on its own merits.
- **Scoring priorities favor this concept regardless of structure:** applications prioritizing housing creation, childcare expansion, **healthy food access**, or **vacant property rehabilitation** receive preference. The healthy-food-market and vacant-reuse angles are worth foregrounding in any application narrative.
- **Sequencing correction:** register the entity *before* requesting the consultation. The consultation is not wasted on early-stage applicants, but showing up with an entity, a defined concept, and a draft project budget converts it from "come back later" into scoring feedback. The plan's original order (consult week 2, form LLC week 3) was backwards.

## 2. The options, honestly weighed

| Structure | What it gets you | What it costs you | Fit now |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Single-member LLC** (MD) | Fast (~1 day online, ~$100 + $300/yr annual report), full control, pass-through tax, SBLP-eligible, owns the building, equity stays with you | No access to donations/foundation grants/charitable programs | **Strong** |
| **Benefit LLC** (MD; same statute family — Maryland pioneered these) | Everything above, plus a stated general-public-benefit purpose baked into the formation documents and an annual benefit report. No tax difference. Mission is legible to community-development lenders, district orgs, and future partners; modest protection of mission if partners/investors ever arrive | The annual benefit report (trivial for a single member); a third-party standard for assessment | **Strong — same cost as LLC, better story · CHOSEN** |
| **Benefit corporation** | As above, corporate form | Corporate formalities and double-tax (or S-corp complexity) for no benefit at this scale | Weak |
| **Nonprofit nonstock corp + 501(c)(3)** | Donations, foundation money, Community Catalyst Grants, volunteers, possible property-tax exemption, mission permanence | **No owner equity — the building's upside leaves your balance sheet permanently.** Requires a real, majority-independent board (there is no prospective board today — this is a personal project). IRS determination takes months. Conflict-of-interest rules complicate every transaction between you and the entity. And the café is the worst-fitting piece: a café open to the general public inside a c3 is typically *unrelated business income* — taxed anyway, and a risk to exemption if it dominates. **The commercial engine is precisely what a 501(c)(3) handles worst.** | Weak *as the operating entity, now* |
| **Cooperative** | Member ownership matches the commons ethos; a natural *end state* | Needs members to exist first; wrong tool for the evaluation phase | Later option |
| **Fiscal sponsorship** (programming layer only) | Charitable receipts without forming anything — fastest hybrid on-ramp | Baltimore's fiscal-sponsor landscape is troubled (Strong City collapse; sponsors folding; transparency criticism in 2024 reporting). Usable, but vet the sponsor (e.g., Fusion Partnerships) like a landlord | Phase 2 tool |

## 3. The recommendation *(confirmed as the decision, Aug 27 2026)*

**Form a Maryland benefit LLC now (plain LLC is an acceptable fallback), and keep the hybrid as a Phase 2 decision.** Reasoning against the three stated considerations:

1. **"Modest margins, little growth" does not, by itself, argue for a nonprofit.** The test that matters is *debt-service coverage plus a modest operator salary* — a lifestyle/commons business with no growth story is a perfectly ordinary LLC; growth potential matters to investors, and there are none. What "little growth" *does* mean: the real financial return in this venture is **building equity on cheap long-term community-development debt plus room revenue**, not operating profit. A nonprofit-first structure hands that one durable return to an entity you don't own, before you know whether the concept works. *(v0.3 reframe confirms this — the rooms-and-building engine is exactly the part that needs owner equity.)*
2. **Partial income is enough** (other income covers the rest; operator commits full-time ~40 hrs/wk — D5). The required owner-comp floor is ~$25–35K, which (a) makes the thin base case survivable while the gates gather evidence, and (b) keeps the mission-fit markets in play. The financial and mission cases converge rather than compete — but the floor is enforced by the precommitted G1/G2 bars, not by temperament.
3. **No board exists, and structure should not precede substance.** The credible community board is the one that grows out of the recurring groups, members, and neighborhood relationships that Phase 1 is designed to build. Forming a c3 now means inventing a paper board; forming one in Phase 2 means recruiting a real one. The gift-philosophy instinct — working substance first, institutional form as a consequence — points the same direction.

**The hybrid end-state, made explicit:** the (benefit) LLC owns the building and runs the commercial engine — rooms, café, offices, consignment prints, printing if it advances. A companion 501(c)(3) (or vetted fiscal sponsor, sooner) carries the *library-like free layer* — free programming, **the lending collection and commons/books budget (plan §6 — its natural first program)**, exhibitions, subsidized community room hours — and receives the grants and donations that layer legitimately attracts (Community Catalyst, foundations). The LLC donates space and money to it; the c3 never touches the café. This puts each revenue type in the structure that handles it best, and it's reversible at every step. Long-term, if the commons thesis proves out, the endgame options stay open: sell or donate the building to the nonprofit, or convert operations to a cooperative — from a position of a working institution, not a hopeful filing.

**The trigger test (two arms — written into G1/G2):**
- **Arm (a):** if diligence shows the base case *cannot* cover debt service plus a modest salary (~$30K) without grants and donations as a **permanent** revenue stream (not acceleration), the venture is factually a charity — the honest structure is nonprofit-first, accepting the board, the equity forfeit, and a rebuilt plan around charitable funding.
- **Arm (b) (v0.3):** if the room program fails as a revenue service at the G1/G2 deposit-backed bars, the commercial remainder (café + offices) must be re-tested against arm (a) before any site commitment — it was reviewed and does not stand alone.
