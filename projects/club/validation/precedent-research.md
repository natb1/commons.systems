# Precedent research — the recreational-space hypothesis (§1a)
*Research memo, Aug 27 2026 (updated same day with public-data verification results and proposed plan adjustments; §5 statuses updated same evening from validation run 1 — see `validation/data.md`). Question: are there precedents in other markets for affordable recreational private meeting space sold by the session? Tangential validation? Or good reasons to believe it can't support a business?*

## 1. Direct precedents — the concept exists and operates in other US markets

**Dungeons & Javas (Colorado Springs, CO)** is the closest analog found: an all-day café (coffee program, cold food, themed drinks, alcohol) with private bookable rooms and gaming tables at **$5–10/hr** (VIP room $10/hr or $30 for a 4-hour block), explicitly marketed for D&D campaigns, meetings, and remote work, bookable in 30-min increments up to a year out. Free walk-in commons alongside paid rooms — structurally almost identical to the v0.3 concept. Notable: their market-clearing room price is *below* the plan's $15–35/hr band, in a metro with comparable-or-lower costs than Baltimore. **(Validation T1, Aug 27: their evening slots observed 100% booked-or-blocked across all 5 rooms 1–5 days out; middays soft — see `validation/data.md`.)**

**Mox Boarding House (Seattle, Bellevue, Portland, Chandler — 4 locations)**: game store + full restaurant/bar + themed private rooms for 8–20 guests, marketed for D&D sessions, game nights, birthdays, team outings. Expanding multi-city — the strongest proof that private rooms are a viable *anchor amenity* for a hospitality/retail compound. Caveat: monetization runs through food & beverage (full-service dining in the rooms), not primarily room rent. **(Validation T1: rooms confirmed at $30/hr peak — Mon–Fri 5pm–close, all day Sat/Sun — a direct comp inside the plan's band.)**

**Knight Moves Café (Brookline, MA)**: board game café with two private rooms at **$150/hr** (includes game-play and outside-food fees, up to 15 people) and a $500/hr full buyout. This validates the plan's *party/private-event* segment (the ~$350 price point), not the price-sensitive recurring-club segment.

**Game stores renting D&D rooms**: Paladins Game Castle (Bakersfield) runs a D&D room reservation system; Red Castle Games (Portland) takes room bookings. Nationally, the incumbent-table claim that game shops structurally can't serve this customer is weakening. **Baltimore-specific (operator observation, Aug 27):** neither local shop has a path to room rentals without expansion — Canton Games has space for only one table; No Land Beyond runs a crowded open floor on a bar/restaurant model, and adding private rooms would be a significant business-model change. The fast-follower threat is therefore low locally; the §1a incumbent row stands for Baltimore even as it weakens nationally.

**Bookable-table cover-charge model at scale**: Snakes & Lattes (Toronto→US chain), Hex & Co. / The Uncommons (multiple NYC locations) run per-person cover fees, reservable tables, and private events profitably in dense markets — proof that people pay to occupy recreational table space, though per-person cover rather than per-room session.

## 2. Tangential validation — private-room-by-the-hour recreation is a proven category globally

- **Hong Kong party rooms**: an entire recognized industry of hourly private rooms for small groups doing exactly the §1a activities — board games, mahjong, karaoke, movies brought from home, birthday gatherings. The mediating variable is housing: HK flats are tiny, so the living room is outsourced.
- **Korea**: SpaceCloud (스페이스클라우드) is a mainstream consumer platform whose top categories are party rooms and practice rooms rented by the hour. Study cafés (pay-by-the-hour quiet space) are ubiquitous and spreading to US Korean-diaspora metros.
- **Private karaoke boxes (KTV)**: global growth including Europe (market ~$6.4B 2024 → ~$9.3B 2033 projected) and US expansion — the cleanest proof that small groups pay by the hour for a private room to recreate in. Typically bundled with F&B.
- **Escape rooms**: US-wide (including Baltimore), sell a private room by the session to 4–10 person groups at $30+/person — session-priced group recreation clears *above* the plan's price band, with programmed content doing the differentiation.

Pattern: **the consumer behavior is abundantly validated.** What varies is what makes the room worth paying for versus a free living room: space scarcity (HK/NYC/Seoul), equipment (karaoke), content (escape rooms), or hospitality (Mox's dining).

## 3. The case against — why it might not support a business

1. **The free substitute is someone's living room.** Baltimore's cheap, large rowhouses weaken the space-scarcity forcing function behind HK party rooms and NYC game cafés. The §1a bet — vibe + neutrality + no-host-burden beats free — is plausible but exactly the unproven part; no found precedent proves it in a low-housing-cost market on room-rent alone.
2. **Price discovery points below the band.** Dungeons & Javas clears at $5–10/hr. If Baltimore clears near $10/hr, the $39K evening base needs roughly double the session count or the price assumption fails. (Counterpoint: local Peerspace floor is $25–50/hr — see §5.2 — so the recreational band likely sits between the two.)
3. **Almost no one monetizes the room alone.** Mox = F&B service; Snakes & Lattes/Hex & Co = per-person cover; game shops = retail attach; karaoke = equipment + drinks. The D15 rule (optional catering, never a minimum, BYO allowed) deliberately refuses the attach mechanisms that make incumbents' rooms pencil — a real tension to resolve with structures that add attach without breaking affordability (§7).
4. **Utilization concentrates.** Demand piles into Thu–Sun evenings; many of the ~25 nominal session-slots/week are structurally unsellable. 33% base utilization may effectively mean near-full weekend booking. **(Validation T1 confirms both halves at the analog: evenings/Saturdays sold out, middays open.)**
5. **Adjacent-category failures.** Geek Retreat (UK gaming-café chain) collapsed from 57 stores (Oct 2022) to ~17. Breather liquidated 400 on-demand rooms in 2020; its CEO: the operator model "never made sense." Both differ in cost structure from a café that exists anyway, but both caution against carrying dedicated room inventory against peaky demand.

## 4. Net read

The hypothesis is not exotic — versions operate today in Colorado Springs, Seattle/Portland, Boston, NYC, and as whole industries in East Asia. But nowhere found does it work as *pure room rent at recreational prices in a mid-density, cheap-housing US market* — every working precedent adds an attach mechanism or rides housing scarcity. The G1 deposit test remains the only proof that matters; the adjustments in §7 exist to raise its odds and its information yield.

## 5. Public-data verification results (Aug 27, 2026) — §1a falsifiable claims

**5.1 Pratt library rooms — §1a claim SUBSTANTIALLY CONFIRMED, and stronger than stated.** Pratt's own policy: small rooms (≤8 people) are free, BUT social events are prohibited ("birthday parties, graduation parties…"), and **recurring bookings require leadership review — "most are denied."** The §1a recurring club is excluded from Pratt *by policy*, not just by crowding. The free competitor has voluntarily exited the recurring-recreational segment. **(Hours arm CLOSED by validation T4, Aug 27: Central, Southeast Anchor, and Penn Ave branches all close ≤8 PM Mon–Thu, 5 PM Fri–Sat, closed Sunday; no branch citywide open past 8 PM on any day. Prime-time overlap: 1 of 4 hours Mon–Thu, 0 Fri–Sun. Both arms of the claim — policy and hours — are now verified.)**

**5.2 Peerspace Baltimore — §1a claim PARTIALLY FAILS as stated, but the gap survives on different grounds.** Current Baltimore meeting-room inventory: 24 of 36 listings under $100/hr, 9 at ≤$50/hr, floor $25/hr. "Rarely under $100/hr" is not true locally. What *does* hold: the sub-$50 inventory is healing-center rooms, conference rooms, coworking, and townhomes — no recreational café/bookstore vibe anywhere in it — with 1–4 hr minimums, per-booking friction, and no standing weekly slot product. **Recommend rewriting the §1a Peerspace row**: the gap is vibe + zero-friction recurrence + F&B-adjacency, not primarily price. Second implication: a $25–35/hr *peak* rate is defensible against local comps; the plan's band does not undercut the market as much as feared.

**5.3 Willingness-to-pay when content is provided — NEW SUPPORTING DATA.** StartPlaying's marketplace average is **$15–20 per player per session** for GM-run games — a 5-player table represents $75–100/session of demonstrated recreational spend, above the plan's per-room band. Programmed content roughly doubles what the same group pays for the same table. **(Validation T5 adds local proof: Canton Games' paid D&D clears $15/seat 3 nights/wk with waitlists; Games & Stuff's premium $75/seat events sell out; a Baltimore GM charges $20/session at 5.0★ — the $15–20/seat mark is proven in-market, at both ends.)**

**5.4 Baltimore game shops (operator observation)** — see §1: no local fast-follower path without expansion.

## 7. Proposed plan adjustments and effect on the numbers

Anchors from §5/§6 of the business plan: evening line $45K base ($39K sessions + $6K catering) ≈ 33% utilization at ~$90 avg session; capacity ~$117K; each filled weekly session ≈ +$4.4K/yr owner comp; LI gap to $30K draw = $43K.

1. **Hybrid pricing: per-person cover with a room floor** (Snakes & Lattes/Hex precedent). E.g., $8–10/person/session with a ~$40 room minimum, instead of flat $15–35/hr. A 4-person club pays $40 (cheaper than flat rate — affordability preserved); an 8-person group pays $64–80. Scales revenue with group size, feels cheaper per head, and matches the only pricing structure proven at scale in this category. Effect: avg session $90 → ~$95–105 on a realistic mix; +$2–7K at base counts; bigger effect is conversion probability.
2. **Cover includes a drink** — attach without a minimum (D15-compatible: included value, not required spend). Per-head cover includes drip/tea; ~70% of the embedded ~$3 is margin and it seeds evening café habit. Effect: ~+$2–4K margin at base volumes.
3. **Ticketed, GM-run program nights** (validated by StartPlaying's $15–20/head and escape-room pricing). Partner with local GMs on rev-share (say 60 GM / 40 house): 5 seats × $18 = ~$90 gross, house nets ~$36 + covers + F&B, in slots that would otherwise sleep — and it *manufactures* recurring groups (strangers meet at a ticketed table, become a club that rents rooms). Two nights/wk ≈ +$4–7K direct, plus the funnel. Same mechanism works as author talks/writing workshops on the book side.
4. **Standing-slot club memberships**: recurring clubs prepay monthly for a locked weekly slot (~10–15% below drop-in). Six clubs at ~$250/mo ≈ $18K/yr contracted — not additive to capacity, but converts the hypothesis into subscription revenue, and turns the G1 instrument from "deposit" into "first month prepaid," a stronger commitment signal.
5. **Equip one room as a media/karaoke room** (~$3–6K capex, KTV precedent): projector/sound converts living-room-substitutable sessions into equipment-differentiated ones (movie nights, karaoke parties, watch parties) at $30–40/hr party-segment rates — defensible against the $50–100/hr Peerspace party comps with a far better vibe. Effect: +$4–8K/yr at 2–3 premium sessions/wk; also the cheapest hedge against pure-RPG demand undershooting.
6. **Peak/off-peak banding**: $10–15/hr Sun–Wed (Dungeons & Javas territory — soaks price-sensitive clubs into dead slots), $25–35/hr Thu–Sat peak (justified by local Peerspace floor). Roughly revenue-neutral at base but flattens the utilization concentration problem (§3.4). *(T1's midday-soft/evening-sellout curve at the analog is direct evidence for this structure.)*
7. **G1 changes**: (a) add price discovery — offer prospective groups two structures (flat vs per-head) and two price points, record which converts; (b) count the §5 demand census toward the rooms bar as a leading indicator, with deposits/prepaid months remaining the bar itself; (c) rewrite the §1a Peerspace row per 5.2.

**Roll-up**: adjustments 1–5 at conservative marks move the evening line from ~$45K to ~**$60–75K** (+$15–30K, most of it near-100%-margin), cutting the LI gap from $43K to roughly $15–30K — which the café covers at ~108–118 tx/day instead of ~123. Nothing here rescues a failed hypothesis: if groups won't book at any structure, G1 still kills it. What the adjustments do is (a) adopt the attach mechanisms every surviving precedent uses, (b) de-concentrate utilization, and (c) make G1 yield *pricing* information instead of a bare yes/no.
